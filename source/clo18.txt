There are yet other types of statistics to test that I have not
already introduced. For categorical variables, where the statistics
are proportions, I've considered the difference in proportions and a
single proportion. You can think of these as trials: a difference in
proportion is for a controled trial, where a control group is compared
against a treatment group; and a single proportion is trial without a
control group where we compare against a threshold.

But what if there are more than just two groups in the trial? What if,
for example, I wanted to study the rate of high school graduation
across several major Canadian cities. There still is success and
failre, graduatation and not graduation. But instead of control and
treatment, I now have many categories: Montreal, Toronto, Winnipeg,
Calgary, Edmonton, Vancouver, perhaps more. This is the situation of
multiple proportions. Each group has a proportion, but there are more
than two groups and I want to compare them all. 

Even more, I could consider various level of education: high school
graduate, dipolma, degreee, graduate degrees, and then compare these
across the same list of cities. I would now get even more proportions:
each city will have a proportion for each level of education, and I
can compare all these against each other.

In this context, I want to do hypothesis testing. But what even is a
hypothesis in this context? It's a bit tricky. Consider, first, some
example data about lizards that we used earlier in the term in a class
activity. 

This is data about where lizards were found resting. There were two
categorical variables, but each had three categories. Here is the
contingency table, to remind you. One variable is the habitat, and one
variable is the typical amount of shade in which the lizards rested. 

There are nine total proportions here. For example, 51.9% of the
mountain habitat lizards were found resting in full sun, and 37.5% of
valley habitat lizards were found resting in partial shade. I could
compare two of these in a difference or proportions, but I want to
know about the difference across all nine. I'm asking a general
question here: how much does habitat affect the choice of sun or shade
in where the lizards rest? This question includes, implicitly, all
nine proportions. The answer I'm looking for is, roughly, binary:
either the habitat has a strong effect or a negligible effect. This is
a setup for hypothesis test.

I want to do hypothesis testing, so I'm going to need some baseline
information in order to setup my null hypothesis. Let me first look at
how many disclosures there were in total. I add up the disclosures and
divide by the number of cases, and I get a general disclosure rate of
27.8%. Mutliplying by 73, this means that if all is equal, there
should be about 20 disclosures in each of the three groups of 73
cases. (Technically, 20.33 disclouse, which I will use later.)
Similarly, I could calculate that there should be about 53
non-disclosure in each group of 73.

The null hypothesis is that the questions make no difference at all,
that in the whole population, roughly 22 out of 73 people would
disclose and the difference between these groups in this sample is
just random. Again, I want to use probability theory to ask how likely
the sample is under this null hypothesis. 

SLIDE

To test the likeliness of our sample, I need a sample statistic.
That's a bit tricky here, with multiple proportions. I want to measure
how much my sample differs from the null hypothesis. I have a baseline
of 20.33 disclosures and 52.67 non-disclosures. I want to compare this
baseline to my sample. I have six situations: three groups and two
options for each group (disclose or not disclose). Each of these six
situation is a proportion. For all of the six, I use the following
formula to measure the difference between the sample outcome and the
baseline I just established. (In this formula, x is the count
from the sample and b is the baseline count from the null
hypothesis.)

Why this formula? It is the difference, which is good since I want to
measure the difference. Squaring the difference means that all
differences are positive, which is again good since I'm not concern
here about the direction of the difference. Then dividing by the null
hypothesis count scales this difference relative to that base. 

I can apply this formula to all six of the proportions in the data.
Here are those calculations.  These are a measure of how much each
proportion differs from the null hypothesis baseline proportion.  Then
I add all these together.  This is a Chi^2 statistic, using the greek
letter Chi. (This is not a roman X, even though it looks almost
identical.) It is the standard way to measure how much a set of
proportions (of any size) differs from a baseline proportion. THis
setup comes from probability theory and that theory, for various
mathematical reasons, has concluded that this is the preferred
measurement. We can all this the total variance in the
sample.

This is the sample statistic. What do we do with it? How do we use
the Chi^2 to answer the hypothesis testing question?  As before, we
can do a randomization process. In this case, the null hypothesis is
that all categories are equally likely. To emulate that, we can just
shuffle the data: take the 61 disclosure and the 158 hide and then just
re-destribute them among the groups.  Then, each of these instances
would have its own Chi^2 value and I could calculate those, one by
one. This would give me a distribution, and then I could compare that
dsitribution with the sample statistic, to see how likely it is. This
is the same randomization process I used previously, just with more
categories to shuffle between.

That would be doing the randomizations by hand. I don't want to do
that. I want to make use of the fact that many clever mathematicians
have build probability models so that I can just calculate directly.
Happily, this is true, but the models are now different. This is no
longer a normal distribution at all. The previous histogram didn't
look normal at all: it started with lots of data and then decayed away
only in the positive direction.  There were no negatives at all
(indeed, Chi^2 is always a positive number).

SLIDE

The theory here, then, it not based on normal distributions. It is
based on another set of mathematical functions, unsurprisingly called
Chi^2 distributions. The normal distributions difference by mean and
standard deviations. For Chi^2 there is one parameter. This parameter
is misleadingly called <term>degrees of freedom</term>. <xref
ref="figure-eight-chi-squared"/> shows the graphs of the
Chi^2-distribution where the degrees of freedom parameter ranges from
1 to 8. All of these distribution start at a high value at zero and
then decay away in the positive direction. They don't exist at all
below zero. 

Like with normal distributions, understanding these mathematical
functions allows us to do the hypothesis test directly, without having
to do the randomizations ourselves. The central limit theorem said
that the sampling distribution for a difference of proprotions (or a
single proportion) is normal under certain reasonable conditions. The
same is true here: there are conditions for the data to fit these
distributions. I'm not going to get into the details of those
conditions<mdash/>a well designed study with good data should satisfy,
but you should be aware that these conditions do exist. 

SLIDE

To use the theory, I need to know how to determine the degrees of
freedom parameter. We are dealing with a categorical variables with
multiple categories. This is actually pretty straightforant: the
number of categories is this degree of freedom. Specifically, take the
two cateogrical variables we have here. Take the number of categories
in the first varible, subtract one, then multiply by the number of
categories in the second variable, again subtrating one.

In our cases, there were three categories of questions, so the first
calculation here is 3-1, and two categories of outcome, so 2-1 in the
second calculation. The degree of freedom we need for this particular
Chi^2 process is 2.
