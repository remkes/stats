    Section 4.1 Linear Models

    The methods of the previous few sections were descriptive statistics: ways to summarize and display information from a data set. Now I want to slowly move into inferential statistics. Instead of just summarizing or displaying, I want to try to use the information in a data set to try to uncover some underlying truth about that data. I want to try to infer something.
    🔗
    There are many ways to do this, and we will devote the remainder of the course to just a few of the techniques of inferential statistics. We are starting with one important large category: regression. This is a category that applies to two numeric variables. A regression puts forward the idea that the two numeric variables are related by a mathematical function, where one is the independent variable and the other is the dependent variable. In principle, any mathematical function can be chosen in a regression, and regression is commonly performed for many such functions: polynomials, trigonometric functions, exponentials functions, and so on. But by far the most common regression is linear regression.
    🔗
    Before stating the definition that I want to work with in this course, let me remind you about the mathematical structure of a line. The most common equation of a line, where \(x\) and \(y\) are the variables, is called the slope-intercept form of the line.
    \begin{equation*} y = mx + b \end{equation*}
    In this setup, \(x\) is the independent variable, the input, and \(y\) is the dependent variable, the output. \(m\) is the slope of the line, showing how steep the line is, with positive slopes for ascending lines, and negative slopes for descending moving lines. \(b\) is the \(y\)-intercept of the line: it shows where the line crosses the \(y\) axis.
    🔗
    This slope-intercept form is the setup for lines that we will use. Now let me give the main definition of the section.
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.1.

    A linear regression or linear model is a line chosen to match two numeric variables in a data set. The linear model is written in slope-intercept form.
    \begin{equation*} \hat{y} = mx + b \end{equation*}
    The variable \(x\) is called the explanatory variable. The variable \(y\) is called the dependent variable. The notation \(\hat{y}\) indicates that the model is an approximation of the data: individual data points are hopefully near the line, but the \(y\) values of the data points don’t exactly match the \(y\) values of the line. The coefficient \(b\) is the intercept and the coefficient \(m\) is the slope.
    🔗
    To elaborate on a previous definition, if the slope is positive, we say that the linear model demonstrates positive linear correlation. Likewise, if the slope is negative, there is negative linear correlation or inverse linear correlation. Positive correlations means an increase in the explanatory variable causes an increase in the dependent variable; inverse correlation is the opposite, where increasing the explanatory variable decreases the dependent variable.
    🔗
    🔗
    For the linear models we will analyze, the slope is by far the most important of the two coefficients. The slope indicates the strength of the effect of the explanatory variable. A high slope means that a small change in \(x\) produces a large change in \(y\text{.}\) Likewise, a small slope means that change in \(x\) have a pretty small effect.
    🔗
    Visually, we fit linear models to scatterplots. Figure 4.1.2 is a scatterplot of two numeric variables in a data set about possums. The \(x\) variable is the length of the whole animal (including the long tail) in centimeters, and the \(y\) variable is the length of just the head, in milimeters. As usual, the dots are the cases, representing the measurements of one specific possum.
    🔗
    Regression looks at a scatte plot and asks: what relationship is demonstrated by this data? In this scatterplot, it looks like their might be linear relationship with a positive correlation. This also intuitively makes some sense: the larger animals can be reasonably expected to also have larger heads.
    🔗
    I want to fit a linear model to this data. Visually, I want to draw a line through this scatterplot that best fits all these data points. Figure 4.1.3 shows the scatterplot with this line. The \(x\) axis is the explanatory variable, in this case, the total length in centimeters.
    🔗
    The specific line that is drawn through the scatter plot has this equation.
    \begin{equation*} \hat{y} = 41 + 0.59x \end{equation*}
    Remember that the hat notation over the dependent variable is there to remind us that this line is an approximation of the data, not a perfect fit to the data.
    🔗
    Now I can interpret the coefficients of this line. 41 is the intercept. On a raw interpretation, this would mean that if the explanatory variable were zero, the output would be 41. In our units, a total body length of 0cm predicts a head length of 41mm. This obviously doesn’t make much sense: a body length of 0 is not actually a possum at all. However, the data here doesn’t really care about (relatively) very small body lengths. The data is studying grown possums, not developmental data. You can see this in the range of the \(x\) variable: all the data is between 75 and 100 centimeres. So there is an interpretation of the intercept here, but it’s not much of a problem that the interpretation isn’t very sensical. The dataset isn’t really concerned with very small \(x\) values. (Of course, other data sets may care about the interceptc coefficient, if the data does include values of the explanatory variable which are near zero.)
    🔗
    The slope coefficient is more useful here. The number \(0.59\) says that for each cm increase in body length, the expectation is that the corresponding length of the head of the possum is 0.59mm larger. In many ways, this coefficient is the relationship between the two variables, showing how changes in one variable are reflected in the other variable.
    🔗
    A linear regression, once found, can be used to make predictions. If I put a body length into the equation of the line, say 90 cm, then the linear regression predicts a 94.1 millimeter long head. These predictions can then be compared against future data. In this way, a linear model is like a hypothesis: something that comes from the data but can be tested in future experiments to determine how well it holds up.
    🔗
    As I have already noted, a linear model is an approximation. The data points are hopefully near the line, but not exactly on the line. That leads to the next definition.
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.4.

    Let \(\hat{y}\) be a linear model of a data set. For each data point, the difference \(y_i - \hat{y}\) is called the residual of the data point.
    🔗
    🔗
    Using the previous example, consider the datapoint \((75, 94.7)\text{.}\) If I put \(x=75\) into the line, it predicts 85.25 mm. The actual value is 84.7. The different, \(y\) minus \(\hat{y}\text{,}\) is the residual for this data point. In this case, the data is -.55 below the line: the residual is -0.55.
    🔗
    Much can be done with residuals, and we will return to them in Section 4.2. For now, though, I can use residuals to answer an important questions. I’ve talked about linear models, but I haven’t yet said anything about how they are actually determined. How is the line and its coefficients discovered?
    🔗
    There are several methods, but the most common is called the method of least squares. Let me sketch the idea. First, I can number all of the data points from 1 to \(n\) where \(n\) is the number of cases in the data set.
    \begin{equation*} (x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \ldots, (x_n y_n) \end{equation*}
    Then, given any line \(\hat{y} = b + mx\) I can calculate the residuals of each data point. I’ll use the letter \(e\) for these residuals (short for the error of the approximation).
    \begin{equation*} e_i = y_i - \hat{y}(x_i) \end{equation*}
    This gives me a list of residuals, each measuring how far the data point is away from the line.
    \begin{equation*} e_1, e_2, \ldots e_n \end{equation*}
    Since the residuals measure the error, I want the error to be as small as possible. There are different ways to measure this, but the most common method looks at the sum of the squares of the residuals.
    \begin{equation*} e_1^2 + e_2^2 + \ldots + e_n^2 \end{equation*}
    For any line, I can calculate this sum of the squares of the residuals. To find the best line, I want the specific line for which this number is as small as possible. Such a line will be considered the best linear model. Since I have a bunch of squares and I want to minimize them, this is called the ‘method of least squares’.
    🔗
    Why do we use squares? There are a number of reasons, some which require more mathematics to discuss. One important effect, though, is that it makes all the errors positive. Residuals can be positive or negative: above or below the line. By squaring them, I get a positive numbers for each error. A second important effect comes from the fact that squaring has a larger effect on larger numbers. Therefore, this method exagerates large errors: points which are very far away from the line. This method is quite sensitive to major outliers, which is desirable in this context.
    🔗
    Finally, let me talk about minimizing this sum. You might be wondering: how is this actually done? Sure, I can make this sum and say that I want a minimum value, but how is the minimum actually calculated? Unfortunately for us, calculating such a minimum is well beyond the mathematics expected in this course. This is a calculus problem, and a fairly difficult calculus problem at that. In general, mostly problems in mathematics that are searching for a minimum or a maximum end up being calculus problems. Since this is not a calculus course, I am not going to go into the minimization methods.
    🔗
    I’ve now covered the basic ideas of linear regression, but I want to introduce one more important idea in this section. The linear model so far had one explanatory variable. It is possible that we want to consider more than one explanatory variable. This leads to the idea of multiple linear regression. I’ll state the definition specifically for three explanatory variables.
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.5.

    Let \(y\text{,}\) \(x_1\text{,}\) \(x_2\) and \(x_3\) be numeric variables ina data set. A multiple linear regression is a linear model where \(y\) is the dependent variable and depends on all three of the other variables. It has this equation.
    \begin{equation*} \hat{y} = b + m_1x_2 + m_2x_2 + m_3x_3 \end{equation*}
    All three of the variables on the rigth side of the equation are called explanatory variable in the mulitple regression.
    🔗
    🔗
    Though I have stated it for three variables, a multiple linear regression could have any number of variables. Notice that there is still only one intercept coefficient, but that each explanatory variable has its own slope coefficient. Each of those slope coefficients measures the way in which \(y\) depends on each of the explanatory variables.
    🔗
    I can no longer graphs a multiple regression, since there are too many variables (hence too many axes) to draw. But the principles are exactly the same: there are still residuals, I still can find a model by least squares, and the coefficients still explains the relationship. I am just allowing multiple variables to have an effect on my dependent variable.
    🔗
    Let me go back to the example of this section. The data set also include variable for the age of the possum as well as the sex. I’d like to put these all in the linear model. Again, the head length is the dependent variable, which I will still call \(y\text{.}\) A multiple regression might look like this.
    \begin{equation*} \hat{y} = 43.5 + 0.56(\text{length}) + 0.30(\text{age}) - 1.66(\text{sex}) \end{equation*}
    Then I can interpret these coefficients. As discussed above, the intercept is not terribly importnat here, but the other three are important. 0.56 means that each centimeter increase in total body length corresponds with a 0.56mm increase in head length. The 0.30 says that each year older corresponds to 0.3mm increase in head length. Finally, there is also the sex variable. This requires some more discussion.
    🔗
    In this data set, unsurprisingly, sex is a nominal categorical varaible, with two categories: male and female. As such, I can’t put it into an equation. However, there is way to solve this problem.
    🔗

    Definition 4.1.6.

    If I have a categorical variable in a dataset and I assign numbers to each category, the resulting numeric variable is called anindicator variable.
    🔗
    🔗
    The assignment of numbers usually arbitrary (though for ordinal categorical variable, we would usually try to preserve the natural order). In this case, the researches made the following assignment of number to categories:
    \begin{equation*} \text{male} \mapsto 0 \text{ and } \text{female} \mapsto 1. \end{equation*}
    This changes the categorical variable into numbers. Now I can interpret the last term in the multiple regression. The coefficient \(-1.66\) only takes effect for female possums. If the case is male, the indicator variable is 0, so this term is multiplied by 0 and has no effect. If the case is female, the indicator variable is 1, so the term subtract 1.66 from the head length. The result is the observation that the female possums of the same age and body length typically have a head length that is 1.66mm shorter than the males.
    🔗
    I said I couldn’t draw multiple regressions, since there are too many variables. However, I can often draw the effects of an indicator variable. Let me show you how. If I ignore the age category and just do regression with the body length and sex variables, I can treat each instance of the indicator variable as a new line. The coefficient \(-1.66\) moves the line for female possums below the line for the male possums. Figure 4.1.7 show the result. In the figure, the cases are colour coded: red for male and blue for female. I get two lines with the same slope (the other slope coefficient is unchanged) but the lines are shifted up or down from each other. If I have more categories, I could likewise have a whole set of shifted lines.
    🔗
    To finish this section, let me summarize with three points. First, there is mathematical way to create a linear regression by the least squares method: finding the best line by minimizing the residuals. Second, the numbers in that line carry statistical meaning: they encode the relationship between the data, at least approximately. Finally, those relationships can be extended to more variables. Extending to other numeric variables is just adding new terms to the equation, but to use categorical variables we have to assignment numbers to them, making them indicator variables. In all this, the coefficients carry the meaning of the relationship.
